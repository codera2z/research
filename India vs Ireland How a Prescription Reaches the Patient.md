# India vs Ireland: How a Prescription Reaches the Patient

*Comparison report, prepared 22 November 2026. Companion to the Ireland report. India findings draw on government, parliamentary, legal and academic sources; items marked "general knowledge" were not verified in this research.*

## 1. Bottom line

In both countries, strong medicines legally require a prescription from a registered doctor. The big difference is **who carries the prescription after the doctor writes it**.

- **India (common case):** the prescription is a document the *patient* holds, on paper or as a photo or PDF. The patient takes it to any pharmacy and usually pays directly.
- **Ireland:** the GP can send the prescription to the patient's chosen pharmacy over **Healthmail**, a secure HSE-approved email system. The patient just collects. The State pays pharmacists for patients on public schemes.

Your friend's description of India is right for the typical case. India is not purely paper, though. Its 2020 telemedicine rules allow e-prescriptions, and its national digital health mission (ABDM) has issued over 93 crore health IDs. What is still emerging is a routine way for pharmacies to pull a prescription from that digital system.

## 3. India in a little more detail

**Legal frame.** The Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940 and Rules 1945 sort medicines into schedules. Schedule H, H1 and X drugs can be sold by retail only on a registered medical practitioner's prescription. Schedule H1 was added in 2013 to curb misuse of antibiotics, anti-TB and some habit-forming drugs.

**Telemedicine rules (March 2020).** Registered doctors may issue e-prescriptions by email or messaging, or transmit them straight to a pharmacy. Medicines fall into List O (over the counter), List A (low risk), List B (add-on for follow-ups) and a prohibited list. Schedule X and narcotic or psychotropic drugs cannot be prescribed by teleconsultation.

**ABDM.** Launched nationally in September 2021, it gives each person a 14-digit ABHA ID. Government and press reports in mid-2026 cite over 93 crore ABHAs and over 100 crore linked records. In June 2026 the Health Minister launched five new platforms, including a drug-identification system meant to work with e-prescription tools. It is built for interoperability, but pharmacy retrieval of prescriptions is not described as routine.

## 4. Why the two designs differ

This is my interpretation rather than a sourced finding.

- **Ireland has a payer in the middle.** Because the HSE reimburses pharmacists for scheme patients, claims already flow through one State system, so adding a prescription channel fits naturally.
- **India's outpatient medicine market is largely private and fragmented.** With no single payer in the loop, the patient-held prescription stayed the default, and digital tools have been built around identity and records first.
- **Scale and connectivity differ.** ABDM's digital divide and trust challenges are acknowledged in Indian analyses.

## Appendix A. Questions a citizen might ask

**Do I have to carry my prescription?** In India, usually yes, as a paper copy or a digital copy. In Ireland, not if your GP sends it by Healthmail; paper is still accepted.

**Can I use any pharmacy?** In India, you pick one when you buy. In Ireland, you name one to your GP so the message can be addressed to it.

**Can I get strong medicines without a prescription?** Not legally, in either country, for scheduled or prescription-only medicines. Indian state regulators have still found shops selling some without one, so the rule is not always followed.

**Can I get a prescription over the phone or video?** Yes in both. India's 2020 telemedicine rules allow it, except for the most tightly controlled drugs. Ireland's GP-to-pharmacy channel was introduced in 2020 partly so patients did not have to visit surgeries during the pandemic.

**Do I need a health ID?** In India, the ABHA ID is optional for buying medicines (general knowledge). In Ireland, no national ID rule for collection was found, though scheme cards are tied to your PPS number.

**Who pays?** In Ireland, the State pays part or all of the cost for people on medical cards, the Drugs Payment Scheme and the Long Term Illness scheme. In India, outpatient medicine costs are largely paid by the patient (general knowledge, not sourced here).
